An Agnostic Reads: Mark 1-8

July 2026

Before we start this series we would like to start with a short introduction on what to expect.

This series follows our journey through the New Testament. Like we’ve said before, one of us knows these texts very well and lives by them. The other is searching and asking questions.

We start with Mark. It's the shortest of the Gospels, likely the oldest, and the writing style is direct. Everything moves fast. The tension stays high. It tries to answer a question: who is Jesus, and who is he supposed to be? The answer is not as simple as it seems. The people who follow him don’t understand, and the people who oppose him see only threat. And Jesus himself keeps telling people not to reveal who he is. This is why it seemed like the perfect starting point.

We’re not here to convert or debunk. We’re here to read, ask questions, and share our thoughts. Sometimes the two of us will agree, other times we will disagree. That's the point.
If you're a believer, you might find this frustrating. If you're a sceptic, you might find it too generous. We're trying to be honest.

Let's begin.

Mark 1-8

The first half of Mark is filled with a number of recurring themes. We follow Jesus as he casts out demons, heals the sick, and performs various miracles. Throughout all this, he teaches the people gathered around him, but they are not allowed to tell anyone who they think he is. This is known as the Messianic Secret. Why the secrecy? Because "Messiah" meant something specific in first-century Israel: a king, a liberator, a warrior who would overthrow Rome. That's not what Jesus came to do. And so the title becomes a problem; it becomes misunderstood. Jesus came to teach and to suffer for us.

This fits well with another recurring theme. Jesus constantly asks his apostles if they still question their faith, despite what they hear and the miracles they see.

Jesus tells of a sower who scatters his seed on different pieces of ground.
This parable, together with Mark 4:11-12, sparked a question and discussion between the two of us. Does Mark say that as an outsider you cannot enter? Do you always remain an outsider in faith in Jesus? Our conclusion: The types of ground in the story of the sower are different phases in which you can find yourself, and this is therefore adaptable. As soon as your foundation is open to understanding Jesus' word, it will take root and multiply like grain. It is a call to action, not a judgment. For us, therefore, the sharing of the loaves among thousands of people simply stands for “the word” with which everyone is fed. The baskets left over by the apostles represent the abundance that will always be there. And yet, the apostles continue to doubt.

Jesus' response to this doubt in Mark 8:21 speaks volumes. In Mark 8, we also see a change in tone. After everything in the first chapters, Jesus asks his apostles directly: “Who am I?” Peter gives the correct answer: "You are the Messiah." But afterward, Jesus tells them what that means: suffering, rejection, death. Peter protests; this cannot be right. This is not the Messiah he expected.
Jesus' reaction is brutal: "Get behind me, Satan."

Three verses. From confession to rebuke. From "you know who I am" to "you stand against everything I have come for." The disciples have seen everything: miracles, healings, loaves for thousands. And yet, they do not understand it. They want a king. Jesus offers a cross.

That is where Mark 1-8 leaves us. With a question:
If even Peter didn’t understand, did anyone?
